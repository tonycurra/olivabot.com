Gibbon Bot

An ape-like robot for trees. Olivabot started with labor shortages in agriculture, and the need of a mobile lightweight structure for regenerative farming. After many architectures, we focus on the gibbon — smallest of the apes.

Intro

The toughest job is to get olives down. Given the centenary tall olive trees, an ape-like robotics architecture could climb and be efficient in harvesting and inspection. That is the idea behind our arboreal robots: work in the canopy, not from heavy machines on the ground.

So: arboreal robots. After many architectures, Olivabot focuses on the gibbon. Gibbon Bot mk.1 acts as proof of concept. Mk.2 is the architecture we believe in — four robotic arms, two for gibbon arms and two for gibbon legs — a low-cost robotic solution for arboreal locomotion and operations.

Gibbon Bot mk.1

Designed and conceived in May 2025, prototyped and built in June to August 2025, tested in olive trees in August 2025.

This structure has 4 robotic arms of 6–7 axis each, for enhanced mobility. It uses microcontrollers and decentralized reasoning for each arm (and leg) to move and reason independently, and a centralized unit to coordinate and control. Geared DC motors, lithium-ion batteries, ESP32 and nRF microcontrollers that communicate via Bluetooth Low Energy for secure and fast operations. 3D-printed parts in bioplastics and bioinspired fingers for passive gripping. This architecture in mind for efficient long battery operations.

It was long, precise manual work — many fingers stuck and pinched in between gears many times.

The main problem of Gibbon Bot mk.1 is that those motors can’t deliver enough torque for smooth movement on the tree, creating big limits on this architecture’s potential. Problems that we solve with Gibbon Bot mk.2.

Gibbon Bot mk.2

Here, instead of geared DC motors (that can be easily powered from 3–6 volts) we used waterproof, powerful high-torque servo motors.

In a first prototype we tested the potential with off-the-shelf servo motors, powered in a not so safe manner via high-discharge Li-Po battery — the one you’d use for RC cars and planes. Given risks for electricity for humans, and having validated motor types and torque, after long research we found off-the-shelf robotic arms: Waveshare RoArm-M3 Pro. We modified them to be easily powered with 3S Li-Po batteries.

This structure is an assembly of 4 robotic arms — 2 used for gibbon arms and 2 for gibbon legs. It still requires a lot of work to be tested in the field, and our funding is running low. Even so, this architecture is terribly promising as a low-cost robotic solution for arboreal locomotion and operations.

What next

We believe in this vision. After years of rapid prototyping and high-energy / low-income hard technical work, we are unsure of when we will be able to deliver this field-tested prototype to the world.

Maybe we will release our work in open source for passionate people around the world to be able to continue — our funding makes it difficult to keep going this work without salary. Maybe we will continue on the side of other jobs, but it will take time.

The future of agriculture looks bright. Patience and vision will help communities worldwide to produce food and manage groves, forests, orchards, in a sustainable manner, aligning with the UN’s Sustainable Development Goals.

This platform grew out of our arboreal robots R&D. Olivabot Vision is the work on giving the same robot cameras and on-board AI.

If you have trees, a workshop, or a way to help this keep going — field trials, parts, or just wanting to talk — reach out.
