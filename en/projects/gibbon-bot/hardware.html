Gibbon Bot hardware

A quote for a light, gibbon-like robot that can hang in a tree. Send the price first. CAD and a URDF come next. A physical robot only if the simulation behaves and we can pay for one unit.

This is not a purchase order. Gibbon Bot does not work yet. The laboratory code is public: github.com/tonycurra/gibbon-bot.

How to answer

  1. Email gibbon@olivabot.com with three prices: CAD and a URDF, one assembled unit, and ten units. Lead time on each.
  2. If the quote fits, send STEP files and a URDF of the hardware you would actually build. Joint names, axes, limits, effort, mass, and inertia. The files have to match the metal, not a generic arm.
  3. We train that URDF in our own simulator.
  4. If the behavior looks right, and one unit is a price we can pay, we order that unit and test it on a tree.
  5. Ten units are a wider test after that. Quote them now so the number is on the page. We are not ordering ten today.

The body

Four limbs on a short torso. Two arms, two legs, the same length. It hangs and climbs. It is not a biped.

Arms and legs in the current laboratory model are the same kinematic chain (a RoArm-M3). Use these lengths as the size to quote against. They are joint offsets from that model, not a manufacturing drawing.

Limb segment lengths in the laboratory model
Shoulder, base to pitch52 mm
Upper arm237 mm
Forearm145 mm
Wrist to the hand jointabout 110 mm
Chain, shoulder base to hand jointabout 544 mm

The torso in the same model:

Torso size in the laboratory model
Length, hips to shouldersabout 290 mm
Arm mounts, left to right342 mm
Leg mounts, left to right200 mm
Arm mounts ahead of the leg mountsabout 320 mm
Torso shell thicknessabout 98 mm

A supplier may move a few centimetres to save weight or to fit a battery. Say what you changed.

Limbs, hands, head

  • Six revolute joints on each limb, then one end effector. Four limbs.
  • Hands and feet are the same part, four times, to keep the cost down. They are prehensile: they close around a branch, not a flat pad.
  • A head with two joints: turn left and right, tilt up and down. The current lab robot has no head. Put the cameras there.

The structure has to stay light. One hand or one foot should be able to hold the whole robot on a branch while the other limbs move. If it is heavy, the motors spend their torque on the chassis and the climb fails. That is what happened with the first motors.

Motors

The laboratory arm’s model lists these effort limits. They describe the arm we already have. They are not the torque budget for the new machine. Mk.1 could not move smoothly on a tree because the motors were too weak.

Effort limits in the current laboratory limb model
Shoulder, elbow, wrist joints2.94 N·m each
Shoulder lift5.88 N·m, two motors on one joint
Gripper1.96 N·m

Size the new motors yourself for a robot hanging from one grip, and write down the mass you assumed. Give continuous torque and peak torque per joint. Waterproof, or say how you keep rain out of the joint.

Materials and battery

  • Structure: aluminum alloy, stainless steel, or metal sheet where a little give helps the grip. Keep it light.
  • Where a part touches bark, metal or a bioplastic. The point is to leave as little pollution on the tree as possible. Say which surfaces are which.
  • Battery in the torso: lithium-ion or sodium-ion. Voltage, capacity, and where it sits. The bench pack on mk.2 was a 3S Li-Po. Do not copy that for a quote.

What to put in the email

  • Price and lead time for CAD (STEP) and a matching URDF.
  • Price and lead time for one assembled unit, for a lab test and then a tree.
  • Price and lead time for ten units.
  • Mass of one robot, limbs and torso separate if you can.
  • Motor torques, and the hanging case you sized them for.
  • Battery chemistry, voltage, and capacity.
  • Materials, especially anything that touches the tree.
